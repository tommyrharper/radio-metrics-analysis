**Bibcode:** 2005sf2a.conf..721P
**Authors:** Jérôme Pety, on behalf of the GILDAS team
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2005sf2a.conf..721P/abstract
**Full text:** https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234489147_Successes_of_and_Challenges_to_GILDAS_a_State-of-the-Art_Radioastronomy_Toolkit
**Institutional record:** https://web-archives.iram.fr/ARN/dec05/node12.html

## One-line summary
This descriptive software paper presents GILDAS as IRAM's cross-platform single-dish and interferometric reduction toolkit, documents its architecture and 2005 modernization, and reports operational capabilities rather than a controlled imaging-fidelity benchmark.

## Method
GILDAS is organized around the GREG kernel, which provides shared services such as graphics, plus astronomy packages including ASTRO, CLASS, CLIC, and MAPPING. The code was available on Linux, UNIX, Mac OS X, and Windows; most of it was written in Fortran 90, with keyboard, plotting, and widget interfaces in C/C++. At IRAM it was the default reduction environment for the 30 m telescope, except bolometer observations reduced with MOPSIC, and for the Plateau de Bure Interferometer, except VLBI observations.

The paper describes software-engineering changes made during the preceding three years: a reorganized source tree with named maintainers, CVS version control, a GNU make build, centralized web and email support, and a Java web interface for ASPRO. The transition retired Fortran 77 support and the GRAPHIC program, moved its functions into GREG or MAPPING, and replaced older FITS interfaces. Work in progress included 64-bit processor support, Cygwin support on Windows, GNU Fortran 95 compiler support, and updated kernel documentation.

Instrument-driven changes included a new 30 m control interface named PAKO, a new raw-data format, and a MIRA pre-calibration program for reading raw data, applying atmospheric calibration, and writing CLASS-format data. The 18-pixel, 1 GHz-bandwidth HERA receiver motivated a Fortran 90 rewrite of CLASS to improve On-The-Fly observing support and frequency-to-velocity conversion for wide-band observations. For the Plateau de Bure Interferometer, GILDAS supported a calibration and filtering pipeline, 22 GHz water-vapor-radiometer phase correction, and preparations for new dual-polarization receivers and wider instantaneous bandwidth.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Operational mapping throughput:** with the fully commissioned 18-pixel HERA receiver operating in the 1 mm atmospheric window, the paper reports that 0.1 square degree could be observed in 8 hours and yield about 4,500 independent spectra after processing. This is an instrument-plus-processing workload characterization, not a comparison against another software package.
- **Pipeline project coverage:** the first Plateau de Bure calibration and filtering pipeline is said to give satisfactory results, specifically for instrument monitoring, in 80% of observed projects. The paper does not define "satisfactory," identify the project sample, report an error statistic, or provide a competing baseline.
- **Angular-resolution context:** civil work doubled the longest Plateau de Bure baseline, which the paper states would improve resolution by a factor of two to as fine as 0.3 arcsec at 230 GHz. This is an array-upgrade specification, not measured GILDAS reconstruction fidelity.
- **Atmospheric phase-noise diagnostic:** Figure 2 plots phase noise versus baseline length at 1 mm and 3 mm before and after correction from 22 GHz water-vapor radiometers; raw and residual values are extrapolated to the longest planned baseline. The text and caption give no numerical phase-noise values, aggregate error, or uncertainty, so none is inferred here.
- **Planned bandwidth scaling:** new receivers were expected to increase instantaneous bandwidth from about 600 MHz to 4 GHz. This is a stated future instrument requirement rather than a completed runtime or accuracy result.
- **No controlled software benchmark:** the paper reports no image-domain error, flux-recovery error, dynamic range, residual statistic, runtime, memory use, compute hardware, dataset-level algorithm comparison, or deconvolution benchmark. Its assessment of robustness and satisfactory operation is qualitative apart from the operational figures above.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
The paper establishes the surrounding classical software system: CLIC handles interferometric calibration, MAPPING covers imaging and deconvolution, and GREG supplies common infrastructure. It therefore provides historical deployment, data-volume, calibration, and maintainability context for a visibility-to-image pipeline. However, it does not specify gridding kernels, inverse FFT implementation, deconvolution algorithms, visibility-domain loss functions, or reconstruction-quality criteria, and it contains no machine-learning component. Its most useful lessons for an ML pipeline are systems-level: preserve calibrated-data compatibility, scale to wider-band and multi-pixel instruments, and evaluate phase correction and operational coverage in addition to image quality.
