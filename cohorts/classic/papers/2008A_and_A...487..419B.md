# Correcting direction-dependent gains in the deconvolution of radio interferometric images

**Bibcode:** 2008A&A...487..419B
**Authors:** S. Bhatnagar, T. J. Cornwell, K. Golap, J. M. Uson
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008A%26A...487..419B/abstract
**Full text:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/0805.0834
**arXiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/0805.0834
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361:20079284

## One-line summary
The paper introduces the A-projection framework for incorporating known, time-varying direction-dependent antenna gains into iterative radio-interferometric imaging, reducing primary-beam and polarization-squint systematics by about an order of magnitude in VLA simulations and 1.4 GHz observations.

## Method
The method embeds a baseline-, polarization-, time-, and direction-dependent Sky Mueller matrix in the interferometric measurement operator. The Mueller matrix is formed from per-antenna Jones matrices whose diagonal terms describe the two polarized voltage patterns and whose off-diagonal terms describe polarization leakage. Fourier transforms of these antenna aperture terms provide finite-support convolution functions for gridding and degridding.

Each deconvolution major cycle predicts model visibilities with an accurate forward transform, subtracts them from the observations, and grids the residual visibilities with the conjugate aperture convolution function before an FFT and image-domain determinant normalization. A conventional minor-cycle operator updates the sky model, and the process repeats to a residual-based stopping criterion. The construction is patterned on w-projection and relies on the Sky Jones matrix being approximately unitary, allowing its adjoint to serve as an approximate inverse. Antenna pointing offsets are represented as phase gradients across the aperture, while polarization squint and rotation of an azimuthally asymmetric primary beam are handled through convolution functions evaluated as a function of parallactic angle.

The implementation was exercised in CASA. It corrects known primary-beam, polarization, and pointing-error terms during deconvolution, but the real-data reduction did not jointly solve for unknown pointing errors and self-calibration gains. Full Jones-matrix imaging is estimated to require four times the computing load of conventional imaging that neglects primary-beam effects. The paper argues qualitatively that the FFT-based method scales better than source-by-source peeling or direct Fourier prediction, but it reports no wall-clock, throughput, memory, or compute-hardware benchmark.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **VLA simulation setup:** CASA simulated 1,000,000 visibilities for 27 VLA C-array antennas, a 3 km maximum baseline, 10 s integrations, one channel at 1.4 GHz, and 1 mJy noise per visibility sample over a parallactic-angle range listed as 183 to 45 degrees. The NVSS-derived sky contained 74 point sources from 195 to 2 mJy. A 10 degree parallactic-angle increment modeled rotation of the right- and left-circular beams. The added visibility noise corresponded to about 1 microJy/beam image RMS.
- **Simulated polarization-squint correction:** without correction, the Stokes V image had an approximately 2 mJy peak and 10 microJy/beam RMS. With the proposed correction it was noise-like at approximately 1 microJy/beam RMS, a factor of about 10 improvement. The pointing-error test drew per-antenna mean offsets uniformly between -25 and +25 arcsec with 5 arcsec RMS and used a non-azimuthally symmetric VLA aperture model. Figure 4 reports off-source RMS of 10 microJy/beam without squint and pointing correction and 1 microJy/beam with both corrections; elimination of the source-associated artifacts is assessed visually rather than with a separate reconstruction score.
- **Observed VLA data:** the real test used 1.4 GHz VLA observations of IC2233 totaling about 11.6 h in two passes. Eleven line-free channels of about 24 kHz each were used. Two bright sources, approximately 854 and 145 mJy/beam, lay at about 75% and 35% primary-beam response, respectively. The assumed common antenna aperture model was sampled every 1 degree in parallactic angle. The expected thermal noise was about 0.13 mJy/beam.
- **Observed residual peaks and RMS:** without time-varying primary-beam and polarization-squint correction, Stokes V residual extrema were -9.7 and +2.8 mJy/beam. After correction, extrema were about +/-0.5 mJy/beam, were no longer correlated with the bright-source positions, and the RMS was 0.15 mJy/beam, slightly above the expected thermal noise. The paper characterizes the systematic-error reduction as one order of magnitude and reports that instrumental Stokes V was removed to better than 10% accuracy. Remaining errors are attributed plausibly, but not conclusively, to beam-model error, uncorrected pointing error coupled to self-calibration, and image pixelization.
- **Primary-beam error model:** for a 1 Jy source in the 1.4 GHz VLA C-array data, a measured 40% instantaneous PSF sidelobe and peak primary-beam mismatch of 0.005 predict about 2 mJy peak Stokes I residual and about 10 mJy Stokes V residual. With a piecewise-constant beam approximation sampled every 10 degrees in parallactic angle, predicted peaks are about 1 mJy in Stokes I and 5 mJy in Stokes V. Requiring approximation error to remain several times below a roughly 0.1 mJy/beam thermal sensitivity led to a 1 degree sampling requirement for this data set. These values are analytic estimates tied to the measured PSF and modeled beam, not separate end-to-end timing or fidelity benchmarks.
- **Other reported limits:** first-order polarization leakage reaches about 1e-2 of a diagonal Mueller term and second-order leakage about 1e-4, motivating full-Mueller treatment above dynamic ranges of a few thousand. The discussion estimates that direction-dependent effects and pixelized sky models can limit full-beam imaging dynamic range to about 1e4 to 1e5. The conclusion reports an approximately 1e5:1 pointing-error limit in a typical simulated 1.4 GHz EVLA field away from strong sources. No PSNR, SSIM, source-catalog completeness, runtime speedup, or hardware-normalized performance is reported.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
A-projection directly spans the visibility-to-gridding boundary: known direction-dependent instrumental physics is encoded in per-visibility convolution functions before the regular-grid iFFT, while the adjoint operator carries residuals back into the image used by the deconvolution loop. It is therefore a strong classical measurement-operator baseline for an ML pipeline and a reminder that a learned deconvolver cannot recover information reliably if primary-beam rotation, polarization leakage, or pointing errors are omitted upstream.

The paper's most transferable evaluation pattern is to compare source-associated residual peaks and off-source RMS both before and after correction, preserve their relation to the expected thermal floor, and state the source fluxes, beam positions, array configuration, frequency, integration, and sampling approximation. An ML replacement for gridding or deconvolution should also separate performance on known simulated corruptions from performance on real data with uncertain beam and calibration errors. This paper supplies no compute benchmark, so it supports fidelity and robustness comparisons but not runtime claims.
