## One-line summary
WSClean is a generic wide-field radio imager that corrects non-coplanar-baseline effects with w-stacking, optionally uses rephased w-snapshots, and is shown on MWA data and simulations to image faster than CASA w-projection with comparable or slightly better residual and source-flux accuracy.

## Method
WSClean groups visibilities into uniformly spaced w-layers, convolutionally grids each layer in the uv-plane, performs a two-dimensional inverse FFT per layer, applies the layer-dependent image-domain w-phase correction, sums the corrected images, and applies the final normalization. Prediction reverses these steps so that Cotton-Schwab major cycles can subtract model visibilities accurately. For equally polarized baselines, conjugate symmetry allows the negative-w contribution to be obtained from the positive-w image, halving the required number of layers.

The C++11 implementation reads CASA measurement sets and writes FITS images. Reading, gridding, FFTs, and Hogbom minor-cycle operations are multi-threaded. It supports separate-polarization or joint-polarization cleaning, makes multiple passes over the data when all w-layers do not fit in memory, and uses a seven-pixel Kaiser-Bessel anti-aliasing kernel by default.

For very wide fields, the paper describes two related extensions. Full-sky MWA imaging splits an observation into short snapshots, changes each phase centre to zenith to reduce w-values, images the snapshots, and combines them. For smaller regions far from zenith, WSClean can phase-rotate the visibilities toward zenith, perform w-stacking there, and recenter the output toward the requested direction. The latter is a w-snapshot implementation that avoids FFT-based image warping but requires an additional regridding step before snapshots are combined. The paper also implements image-domain correction of the common full-polarization MWA tile beam from the four instrumental-polarization images.

An additional optimization performs inversion at the Nyquist resolution set by the synthesized beam, interpolates the dirty image to the requested higher-resolution CLEAN grid, and later decimates the high-resolution component model before visibility prediction. This reduces the cost of the many FFTs required by w-stacking while retaining the requested deconvolution grid.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Controlled accuracy benchmark:** 100 sources of 1 Jy were simulated over a 20-degree-diameter MWA field without system noise and imaged with uniform weighting and Cotton-Schwab cleaning to 10 mJy. The primary fidelity measures are source-flux standard error measured with AEGEAN and RMS flux density in the residual image. At zenith with 12 w-layers or planes, WSClean reports 1.31% flux error and 0.94 mJy/beam residual RMS versus CASA's 1.34% and 1.90 mJy/beam. With 128 layers or planes, the corresponding values are 1.39% and 0.94 mJy/beam for WSClean versus 2.08% and 0.94 mJy/beam for CASA. At 10-degree zenith angle with 195 layers or planes, WSClean reports 1.75% and 0.90 mJy/beam, recentered WSClean 1.40% and 1.03 mJy/beam, and CASA 2.41% and 1.07 mJy/beam. Across these tests, the paper summarizes WSClean as having 2-33% lower source-flux errors and 0-49% lower residual RMS than CASA. The authors caution that equal numbers of layers and planes disadvantage CASA at zenith: raising CASA from 12 to 128 planes matches WSClean's residual RMS but not its source-flux error.
- **End-to-end accuracy-test wall time:** The same Table 3 tests used five major iterations. WSClean versus CASA took 8.5 versus 19.3 min at zenith with 12 layers or planes, 10.3 versus 19.6 min at zenith with 128, and 15.3 versus 178.2 min at 10-degree zenith angle with 195. Recentered WSClean took 6.6 min in the 10-degree case.
- **Imaging-only wall-clock benchmark:** Each configuration was run five times and timed for production of the synthesized PSF and image, excluding cleaning, prediction, and the reduced-resolution inversion optimization. Error bars in Figure 7 are five standard deviations, and run-to-run variation was typically a few seconds. For large visibility sets, WSClean was 7.9 times faster than CASA at 10-degree zenith angle and 2.6 times faster at zenith; for small sets it was about 3 times faster. Differences exceeded an order of magnitude at zenith angles of about 20 degrees or more. CASA could not complete several large-w configurations because its w-kernels became too large.
- **Benchmark hardware and software:** Tests used WSClean 1.0 and CASA stable release 42.0 revision 26465 on a desktop with 32 GB RAM and a six-core 3.20 GHz Intel Core i7-3930K rated at 138 GFLOPS. The input data were on five spinning disks with about 450 MB/s aggregate read speed. The common data set had 3.5 x 10^8 visibilities, 768 channels over 30.72 MHz, 2 s time resolution, and a 112 s observation duration.
- **Reduced-resolution inversion speed and fidelity:** Reducing a 3072-pixel inversion to about 1500 pixels saved roughly a factor of 2 at 10-degree zenith angle, and at 10,000 output pixels reduced cost by about an order of magnitude. Recreating the real-data field of Figure 3 lowered wall time from 41 to 24 min; the two residual images differed by 18 mJy/beam RMS against 67 mJy/beam residual noise and a 10.3 Jy/beam peak. The conclusion reports a 2-10 times speedup with no noticeable loss of accuracy in simulations reaching about 1:1000 dynamic range or in that real field.
- **Runtime-model fit error:** Least-squares formulae fitted to the WSClean and CASA timing measurements had absolute errors of 14.8% and 20.7%, respectively.
- **Cross-array cost projections:** For one hour of data at 20-degree zenith angle, one polarization, five major iterations, and approximately equal accuracy, WSClean was predicted to be 2-3 times faster than CASA in most configurations and about 7, 8, and 12 times faster for full LOFAR, MWA/GLEAM, and full SKA1 low-frequency configurations, respectively. The full SKA1 aperture-array case was estimated at 48.8 years on the test desktop for WSClean versus 643 years for CASA w-projection, corresponding to about 59-60 PFLOPS for real-time processing with the modeled current techniques. These are extrapolations from the fitted timing functions, not measured SKA runtimes.
- **Qualitative capability checks:** Figures demonstrate almost-full-sky MWA imaging, image-domain full-polarization beam correction, and a Stokes V pulsar detection. These are capability demonstrations rather than controlled numerical comparisons. The paper does not report PSNR, SSIM, or a common image-similarity score.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
WSClean is a direct classical reference for the gridding and iFFT stages: it replaces w-projection's large visibility-domain kernels with layered gridding, repeated two-dimensional iFFTs, and image-domain w-phase corrections. Its evaluation separates source-flux error and residual RMS from wall-clock cost, identifies runtime hardware and scaling variables, and records whether a speed optimization changes reconstruction fidelity. Those choices form a useful baseline for ML methods that replace gridding, Fourier inversion, or CLEAN: comparisons should preserve the same sky and visibility model, report residual and photometric accuracy as well as runtime, distinguish measured performance from extrapolation, and state the effect of wide-field geometry and direction-dependent corrections.

The deconvolution component in this 2014 version remains classical Hogbom/Cotton-Schwab CLEAN rather than ML, and the paper explicitly did not yet provide multi-scale CLEAN. Its principal contribution is therefore an efficient, interpretable visibility-to-dirty-image operator and major-cycle prediction path against which a learned gridding or reconstruction module can be tested.
