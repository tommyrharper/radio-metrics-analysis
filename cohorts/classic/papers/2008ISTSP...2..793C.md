## One-line summary
Multiscale CLEAN extends CLEAN's greedy component search from delta functions to a simultaneous choice among compact components at several user-specified scales, improving convergence, integrated-flux recovery, and reconstruction error for extended emission while retaining CLEAN's residual update and restoration structure.

## Method
The paper models the sky as a sum of shifted, scaled components \(I_q m(x-x_q,y-y_q,\alpha_q)\), where \(\alpha_q\) is selected from a short list of scale sizes. For each scale, the algorithm convolves the current residual image with the scale kernel and finds its peak. It multiplies each peak by a small-scale preference \(S(\alpha)=1-0.6\alpha/\alpha_{\max}\), selects the largest adjusted peak across all scales and positions, adds that component to the model with the CLEAN loop gain, and updates every scale-convolved residual. This simultaneous search differs from Multi-Resolution CLEAN's sequential progression through resolutions and allows an initially incorrect scale choice to be corrected in later iterations.

The implementation precomputes the point-spread-function response to each component scale, \(B*m(\alpha_q)\), and every pairwise scale cross-term, \(B*m(\alpha_q)*m(\alpha_r)\). Each iteration then reduces to peak finding plus scaling, shifting, and subtracting precomputed images. Iteration stops at a residual threshold or component-count limit. The restored image is the model convolved with a clean beam plus the final residual image.

Components are tapered, truncated parabolas multiplied by a prolate spheroidal wave function. A zero-size component is always included as a delta function. Compact support makes scale-dependent CLEAN windows possible: the allowed center window is trimmed so that an extended component cannot leak beyond the requested support. The author generally uses geometrically spaced scales, terminating at or below the largest expected source scale, but identifies both scale selection and the fixed bias toward small scales as arbitrary design choices. Unlike delta-function CLEAN, the multiscale method is reported to remain usable with loop gain 0.5 or higher. It was implemented in AIPS++/CASA.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Real VLA data:** The real-data example is one H I spectral channel of NGC 1058 observed with the VLA in D configuration. The dirty-image peak is about 12 mJy/beam, and the displayed dirty, restored, and residual images use a range of -3 to +3 mJy/beam. Multiscale CLEAN used 1,000 iterations, loop gain 1.0, scales of 0, 1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 pixels, and a stopping threshold of \(4\sigma\). It recovered 3.08 Jy of integrated flux, compared with 0.32 Jy for Högbom CLEAN. The multiscale flux stayed stable when the integration region was enlarged beyond the nominal source, while the Högbom value was strongly region-dependent because of its residual negative bowl. This is a real-data flux and qualitative residual comparison, not a ground-truth fidelity benchmark; no runtime or hardware is reported for it.
- **M31 simulation setup:** AIPS++/CASA simulated VLA C-configuration observations of the standard M31 test image at declination 45 degrees, with 60 s observations every 5 minutes over hour angles -4 to +4 hours. The comparison used Högbom CLEAN, Clark CLEAN, Multi-Resolution CLEAN, Multiscale CLEAN, and maximum entropy. Multiscale CLEAN again used scales 0, 1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 pixels. Error images were computed against the known model after smoothing it with the applicable clean beam. Runtime was measured on a 3.06 GHz Xeon; the paper does not state core count, memory, compiler, or parallelism.
- **M31 quantitative comparison (image-domain RMS error vs smoothed ground truth, not R2D2 RDR):** Table II reports, respectively, deconvolution time, total recovered flux, and RMS error: Högbom CLEAN, 204 s, 1,474 Jy, and 8.8 mJy/beam; Clark CLEAN, 50 s, 1,326 Jy, and 25.6 mJy/beam; Multi-Resolution CLEAN, 147 s, 1,546 Jy, and 14.7 mJy/beam; Multiscale CLEAN, 614 s, 1,495 Jy, and 4.9 mJy/beam; and maximum entropy, 13 s, 1,486 Jy, and 2.5 mJy/beam. Thus Multiscale CLEAN exactly matched the table's 1,495 Jy reference flux and had the lowest RMS error among the CLEAN-family methods, but it was slower than every comparator in this particular measured run. Maximum entropy was both faster and lower in RMS error here.
- **Convergence and failure context:** For the M31 simulation, Clark CLEAN diverged beyond about 300,000 components; at that point it had recovered 1,391 Jy of the full 1,495 Jy. The text describes this as about 7% low, while Table II separately lists 1,326 Jy for Clark. Multiscale CLEAN converged in 5,000 iterations. The author also reports visible source-correlated residual structure for all compared methods except Multiscale CLEAN, although this observation is qualitative. Multiscale CLEAN still exhibited extended-emission fringing from interpolation across holes and edges in the sampled Fourier plane, but at a lower qualitative level than the other CLEAN reconstructions.
- **Source-size sweep:** Further AIPS++/CASA simulations placed modified M31 and M51 models at declination 45 degrees and observed them with the VLA C array at X band in 60 s integrations over hour angles -1 to +1. Source linear size was varied in factors of \(\sqrt{2}\). The reported metrics are recovered-flux fraction and dynamic range, with dynamic range defined as reconstructed-image peak divided by off-source RMS. Four-scale Multiscale CLEAN trials ranged from a point component to maximum scales of 8 pixels, or \(0.027\lambda/D\), through 64 pixels, or \(0.21\lambda/D\). Larger maximum scales improved recovery for the largest sources, but the paper warns that sources near \(0.8\lambda/D\) and \(1\lambda/D\) are beyond what the C array should image accurately.
- **Noise sweep:** Gaussian visibility noise was added to an M51 model scaled to \(0.30\lambda/D\). The theoretical image-plane SNR is defined as the peak of the clean-beam-convolved model divided by image-plane noise. Maximum entropy began significantly overestimating flux at SNR as high as 100, whereas CLEAN, Maximum Emptiness, and Multiscale CLEAN retained accurate flux estimates down to SNR about 4; Högbom CLEAN remained reasonably accurate in integrated flux even below SNR 1. At low SNR, reconstructed dynamic range flattened near 4 as thermal noise dominated, while high-SNR curves began flattening because of deconvolution error. These thresholds are read from and described alongside parameter-sweep figures; the paper provides no timing, uncertainty, or hardware result for the sweep.
- **Mask and scale interaction:** A separate M31 H II-region simulation used a source size of \(0.83\lambda/D\). Recovered-flux plots show better results with a maximum scale about one quarter of the source size and a tight mask. This is a parameter-sensitivity result rather than a generally optimized setting.
- **Qualitative Hydra A check and evaluation gaps:** A simulated VLA observation of a noise-removed Hydra A image used the same observing scheme as the M31 simulation. Restored, residual, and error images compare Clark, Högbom, and Multiscale CLEAN, but no numerical score or runtime is reported. Across the paper there is no PSNR, SSIM, visibility-domain error, source-catalog accuracy, uncertainty analysis, or memory benchmark.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
This paper is a classical baseline for the deconvolution stage after visibility weighting, gridding, and inverse FFT have produced a dirty image and point-spread function. Its multiscale component dictionary and greedy, scale-aware matching step are natural references for learned sparse representations, unrolled deconvolution networks, or learned component proposal modules. The precomputed cross-scale point-spread-function terms also show how an ML component selector can be paired with an exact physics-based residual update instead of learning the complete reconstruction operator.

The evaluation is especially useful for extended-emission ML systems because it separates integrated-flux recovery, image-domain RMS error against smoothed ground truth, peak-to-off-source-RMS dynamic range, convergence, and runtime. A credible learned replacement should preserve the paper's source size, visibility-noise, array geometry, clean-beam, support mask, and hardware context, and should compare against both Multiscale CLEAN and maximum entropy. The paper does not learn or alter visibility gridding or the iFFT, and its arbitrary scale list and scale bias are clear candidates for data-adaptive selection, but the M31 table cautions that improved CLEAN-family fidelity did not imply the lowest runtime or the lowest error among all classical methods.
