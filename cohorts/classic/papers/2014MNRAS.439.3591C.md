# PURIFY: a new approach to radio-interferometric imaging

**Bibcode:** 2014MNRAS.439.3591C
**Authors:** R. E. Carrillo, J. D. McEwen, Y. Wiaux
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014MNRAS.439.3591C/abstract
**Full text:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/1307.4370

## One-line summary
Carrillo, McEwen, and Wiaux introduce PURIFY, a C implementation of radio-interferometric imaging that combines a continuous-visibility non-uniform FFT measurement operator with an SDMM convex optimizer, and show in controlled simulations that its SARA average-sparsity prior gives higher reconstruction SNR than seven other sparsity and total-variation configurations.

## Method
PURIFY formulates deconvolution as a constrained convex inverse problem. For its weighted analysis formulation, it minimizes the weighted L1 norm of the image coefficients in a sparsity dictionary, subject to an L2 visibility-residual bound and image positivity. The SARA configuration uses a concatenation of the Dirac basis and the first eight orthonormal Daubechies wavelet bases, then approximates an L0 average-sparsity prior through a sequence of reweighted L1 problems. The package also supports a Dirac basis, Daubechies wavelets, total variation, and reweighted versions of these priors.

The solver uses the simultaneous-direction method of multipliers (SDMM). It separates the sparsity penalty, visibility-data constraint, and positivity constraint so their proximity operators can in principle run simultaneously, while a conjugate-gradient solve performs the shared image update. For a Parseval sparsity frame such as SARA's concatenated orthonormal bases, the matrix in that update reduces to \(Q = \Phi^\dagger\Phi + 2I\). The implementation stops when the relative change in the sparsity objective falls below \(10^{-3}\) and the visibility residual divided by its noise bound is within \(1 \mathbin{+/-} 0.1\). It sets the SDMM penalty parameter to \(10^{-3}\|\Psi^\dagger\Phi^\dagger y\|_\infty\).

The continuous-visibility sensing operator is implemented as \(\Phi = GFDZB\): \(B\) applies the primary beam, \(Z\) zero-pads the image to an oversampled Fourier grid, \(D\) corrects the interpolation-kernel convolution, \(F\) is an FFT, and the sparse interpolation matrix \(G\) maps grid values to off-grid visibility coordinates. The reported implementation uses a compact-support Gaussian interpolation kernel. The same framework can partition very large visibility sets across compute nodes, apply separate data-fidelity constraints to each partition, and exchange image-sized rather than visibility-sized vectors, but that distributed design was not implemented in the beta release evaluated here.

The experiments compare eight configurations: non-reweighted BP, BPDb8, BPSA, and TV, and their reweighted counterparts RWBP, RWBPDb8, SARA, and RWTV. BP is treated only as a proxy for CLEAN, based on prior work finding similar reconstruction quality for Dirac-basis L1 minimization; BPDb8 is likewise an indirect proxy for MS-CLEAN. The paper does not run either CLEAN implementation directly. Tests use 256 x 256 M31 and 30 Doradus model images, normalized to brightness values from 0.01 to 1, with both compact and extended structure. The visibilities are continuous off-grid Fourier samples generated with a variable-density profile, with the zero-frequency sample removed, identity primary beam, Fourier oversampling factor 2, and complex Gaussian noise at 30 dB input SNR. The sampling density ranges from \(M = 0.2N\) to \(M = 2N\). Each plotted point is the mean of 30 simulations, with one-standard-deviation error bars. A final experiment uses 9,413 points from a simulated Arcminute Microkelvin Imager coverage and a 128 x 128 M31 model.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Reconstruction SNR:** the primary fidelity metric is \(20\log_{10}(\|x\|_2/\|x-\hat{x}\|_2)\), in dB, measured against the known noiseless model image. This is distinct from the fixed 30 dB input visibility SNR, defined as \(20\log_{10}(\|y_0\|_2/\|n\|_2)\). The visibility constraint radius is set from the complex Gaussian-noise variance using a high-probability chi-squared bound with \(2M\) degrees of freedom.
- **Random variable-density coverage sweep:** across \(M/N = 0.2\) to 2, Figure 2 reports mean reconstruction SNR and mean wall-clock time over 30 noise and coverage realizations for each image. SARA has the highest mean SNR throughout both sweeps. For M31, RWBPDb8 is the next-best reported method but remains as much as 4 dB below SARA. For 30 Doradus, TV and RWTV are at most 2 dB below SARA, while BP and RWBP are at least 4 dB below every other method once the coverage exceeds \(0.2N\). These comparisons use synthetic truth and indirect CLEAN proxies, not observed-sky truth or a direct CLEAN run.
- **Representative \(M = 26{,}374 \mathrel{\simeq} 0.4N\) reconstructions:** Figure 3 gives M31 SNRs of 32.4 dB for SARA, 30.6 dB for RWBPDb8, 28.6 dB for RWTV, and 23.4 dB for RWBP. Figure 4 gives 30 Doradus SNRs of 25.3, 22.6, 24.1, and 18.8 dB, respectively. The corresponding reconstructed images, truth-minus-reconstruction error maps, and residual dirty images provide qualitative spatial evidence that SARA leaves fewer background artifacts and smaller errors in structured regions.
- **AMI-coverage reconstruction:** with 9,413 simulated AMI visibility coordinates and the 128 x 128 M31 model, the reported SNRs are 10.7 dB (BP), 10.9 dB (RWBP), 11.6 dB (BPDb8), 12.3 dB (RWBPDb8), 10.6 dB (TV), 10.5 dB (RWTV), 12.4 dB (BPSA), and 14.3 dB (SARA). SARA therefore leads the nearest reported competitor, BPSA, by 1.9 dB in this experiment and also shows fewer visible artifacts. This is a simulated telescope coverage, not reconstruction of real measured visibilities.
- **Runtime and hardware:** Figure 2 measures wall-clock reconstruction time on a 2.4 GHz quad-core Xeon using the non-optimized beta software. Reweighted methods take from tens of minutes at \(0.2N\) to about one hour at \(2N\); their time scales approximately linearly with visibility count because the conjugate-gradient step repeatedly applies \(\Phi\) and \(\Phi^\dagger\). Non-reweighted methods remain below 10 minutes across the sweep except TV on 30 Doradus. RWBPDb8 is the slowest and shows the largest timing error bars, while SARA remains competitive because its nine basis decompositions are parallelized. The beta code otherwise evaluates the SDMM proximity operators serially, so these timings do not benchmark the paper's proposed fully parallel or distributed design.
- **Residual-map assessment:** residual dirty-image flatness is inspected qualitatively, but the paper explicitly warns that it is not an adequate fidelity metric. In the 30 Doradus example, RWBPDb8 produces a nearly flat residual while its truth-based error and SNR are worse than RWTV's. No dynamic range, flux-recovery error, astrometric error, uncertainty calibration, memory consumption, or visibility-throughput metric is reported.
- **Limitations and comparison scope:** all numerical fidelity results use synthetic images with known truth and simulated visibilities. The current implementation does not include complex gain matrices, natural or uniform weighting, direction-dependent effects, or the proposed distributed data partitioning; the paper describes these as straightforward or future extensions. Its claims relative to CLEAN and MS-CLEAN rely on proxy formulations and earlier studies, so the experiments establish differences among PURIFY priors rather than a direct end-to-end benchmark against production radio-imaging software.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
PURIFY gives a precise classical optimization counterpart to the full visibility-to-image chain. Its \(GFDZB\) operator exposes primary-beam application, zero-padding, interpolation-kernel correction, FFT, and off-grid visibility interpolation as separate linear components; its adjoint supplies the dirty-image operation used repeatedly during reconstruction. The SDMM layer then performs noise-constrained, positivity-constrained deconvolution with an explicit sparse or total-variation prior instead of CLEAN's major-minor cycles.

For ML pipelines, this structure supplies both a reproducible non-learned baseline and natural unrolling boundaries: learned modules could replace or tune the sparsity proximal operator, interpolation kernel, conjugate-gradient preconditioner, or regularization weights while retaining a measurement-consistent visibility residual. The paper's strongest evaluation lesson is that a visually flat dirty residual does not guarantee accurate recovery. Truth-based image error, SNR, structured error maps, runtime on named hardware, coverage density, and input-noise level should therefore be reported together. Its scalability design also anticipates modern training and inference constraints by separating data-local visibility operations from image-sized global updates, although that parallel architecture and direction-dependent measurement effects remain proposals rather than benchmarked capabilities in this release.
