# Aperture Synthesis with a Non-Regular Distribution of Interferometer Baselines

**Bibcode:** 1974A&AS...15..417H
**Authors:** J. A. Högbom
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1974A%26AS...15..417H/abstract
**Full text:** https://articles.adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1974A%26AS...15..417H

## One-line summary
Högbom introduces CLEAN as an iterative image-plane procedure that exploits the known dirty beam and the expected sparsity of radio skies to remove sidelobe responses from Fourier-inverted, irregularly sampled interferometer data, then restores the recovered components with an idealized clean beam.

## Method
CLEAN starts from a dirty map and dirty beam computed by standard Fourier inversion of the measured visibilities and their weights. Högbom argues that the dirty map is the true, primary-beam-weighted sky convolved with the dirty beam, and that direct inversion can be unusable when irregular sampling produces extended sidelobes. The method adds the prior assumption that significant emission occupies only a small part of a high-resolution radio map.

At each iteration, CLEAN finds the largest absolute residual-map value, subtracts a shifted dirty-beam pattern whose central amplitude is that value multiplied by a loop gain, and records the removed point component. Iteration stops when the next peak is no longer significant relative to the map noise. The recorded components are then returned to the final residual map at their fitted positions and amplitudes, but convolved with a selected clean beam whose main lobe is matched by the dirty-beam weighting. For equal weights at measured Fourier samples and zero elsewhere, the dirty map itself is the correlation of the dirty map with the dirty beam, avoiding a separate correlation calculation.

The paper also gives implementation heuristics. The peak can be tested against the mean absolute map value using a factor of order 4. Högbom reports little further improvement from reducing the loop gain below about 1/2, although gain 1 works in simple cases. Allowing the gain to vary from 0.25 to 0.5 after normalizing the dirty-map and dirty-beam maxima permits dirty-beam multiplication to be replaced by subtraction and occasional map doubling. These are practical recommendations, not results from a controlled timing experiment.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **No modern scalar image-fidelity benchmark is reported.** The paper defines no SNR, PSNR, normalized error, dynamic range, flux-recovery error, astrometric error, or statistical uncertainty metric, and it gives no runtime, memory, throughput, or hardware measurements.
- **Green Bank qualitative iteration study (3C 244.1 at 2695 MHz):** Figure 3 compares the dirty map with cleaned maps after 1, 2, and 6 iterations, all using loop gain 1. The data contain 50 interferometer baselines, corresponding to 100 measured amplitudes and phases. Högbom states that the six-iteration map can be described by fewer than 30 numbers, and attributes the later visual improvement to removal of sidelobes from weaker features left after two iterations. Contour maps provide the fidelity evidence; no ground-truth error is available.
- **Westerbork sampling-reduction study (3C 130 at 1415 MHz):** Figure 4 compares cleaned maps from 200, 100, 50, and 30 baselines. The 200-baseline case used 10 hour angles at each of 20 spacings and required 3 1/3 hours of instrument time, versus the stated 12-hour minimum for conventional two-dimensional synthesis with that telescope. The 100-baseline map is described as very similar to the 200-baseline map; the 50-baseline map is seriously disturbed but retains the main source features; the 30-baseline map is dominated by confusion and judged insufficient. These are qualitative contour comparisons, not numerical similarity scores.
- **Stopping and reliability criteria:** the suggested peak-significance heuristic is a maximum absolute residual exceeding the mean absolute map value by a factor of order 4. The discussion also frames continuation as requiring more than a 50% probability that a peak is partly due to a real feature, but does not provide a calibrated probability model or validation dataset. Complex sources, receiver noise, and purely random data cause early stopping with residual sidelobe confusion.
- **Wide-field validity bound:** because a three-dimensional baseline distribution makes the synthesized beam position-dependent, fixed-beam CLEAN is restricted approximately by \(w_{\max} p_{\max}^2 \approx 1\), with \(w_{\max}\) measured in wavelengths. For a maximum physical baseline of 10 km at wavelength 6 cm, the paper obtains \(p_{\max} \approx 0.00245\) radians, or about 8 arcmin. This is an analytical applicability limit, not an empirical fidelity score.
- **Limitations:** CLEAN cannot recover emission completely resolved at all measured baselines, and it cannot produce a reliable detailed map when source complexity requires at least as many parameters as there are measurements. Smoothing the residual and dirty beam to lower resolution is suggested for weak extended emission. The paper does not compare CLEAN quantitatively with maximum entropy, positivity-based reconstruction, model fitting, or interpolation.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
This paper establishes the classical deconvolution stage that learned methods commonly replace or unroll: irregular visibilities are weighted and Fourier-inverted into a dirty image and dirty beam, then an iterative residual model removes shifted PSF responses before clean-beam restoration. It also separates gridding from deconvolution explicitly: convolutional interpolation is useful for placing measurements on the rectangular grid required by an FFT, but Högbom warns that it does not eliminate sidelobes caused by missing Fourier coverage and can add aliasing. CLEAN's peak-residual selection, dirty-beam subtraction, stopping rule, sparse component representation, and stated failure modes provide a direct classical baseline for ML deconvolution, while its qualitative evaluation highlights the need for modern pipelines to add reproducible fidelity, residual, runtime, and wide-field metrics.
