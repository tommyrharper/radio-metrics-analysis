# The Noncoplanar Baselines Effect in Radio Interferometry: The W-Projection Algorithm

**Bibcode:** 2008ISTSP...2..647C
**Authors:** T. J. Cornwell, K. Golap, S. Bhatnagar
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ISTSP...2..647C/abstract
**arXiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/0807.4161
**Full text:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/0807.4161
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1109/JSTSP.2008.2005290

## One-line summary
W-projection corrects wide-field non-coplanar-baseline effects by convolutionally projecting measured visibilities onto the w = 0 plane, enabling a single two-dimensional inverse FFT and, at comparable simulated-image dynamic range, roughly an order-of-magnitude lower runtime than uvw-space faceting.

## Method
The paper rewrites the non-coplanar visibility equation as a convolution between the visibility function on the w = 0 plane and the Fourier transform of a w-dependent image-plane phase screen. It interprets that convolution physically as differential Fresnel diffraction between antenna planes. For prediction, the method multiplies a sky model by an anti-aliasing taper, applies a two-dimensional FFT, and convolves the resulting w = 0 visibility plane to each measured (u, v, w) location. For imaging, it applies the transpose operation: each measured visibility is convolutionally gridded onto the w = 0 plane, a two-dimensional inverse FFT produces a tapered dirty image, and division by the taper recovers the dirty image.

The implementation uses a spheroidal image-plane taper. Its w-dependent convolution functions are obtained by numerical integration with four-times image-plane padding, equal spacing of w-planes, and truncation of the aggregate convolution function at 0.1%. Those choices were selected empirically to suppress aliasing to a dynamic range of at least 10^4. More w-planes improve alias control while adding relatively little gridding time, but they increase initialization time and memory. The paper reports that a typical w-projection convolution support can reach about 70 by 70 pixels, compared with 9 by 9 for the ordinary spheroidal gridding function, making gridding about 10-20 times more expensive than an uncorrected two-dimensional transform.

W-projection is implemented in AIPS++/CASA, mainly in C++ with hand-optimized Fortran inner loops for gridding and Fourier transforms. It supplies the accurate prediction and residual-image operations for the major cycle while a minor cycle may use standard CLEAN, multi-scale CLEAN, or maximum entropy deconvolution. A single central point-spread function is used in the minor cycle, which is stopped before its position-dependent approximation errors become significant.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Controlled simulation and evaluation context:** The main benchmark is a full-synthesis 74 MHz VLA C-configuration simulation centered at right ascension 12h56m57.18 and declination 47d20m20.801. It contains 505,440 visibility records with eight spectral channels each. The sky comprises 66 WENSS sources brighter than 2 Jy within 12 degrees of the field center, scaled to 74 MHz with spectral index -0.7 and multiplied by a simple 74 MHz primary-beam model. The visibilities were generated with analytic transforms to machine precision; the brightest input source is stated as 47.8 Jy. The authors identify the absence of weak sources as the simulation's main realism limitation.
- **Hardware, software, and deconvolution context:** Processing used a dual 3.06 GHz Xeon machine with 512 kB cache per processor and 3 GB memory. AIPS++ was compiled with GNU compilers at optimization level O2. Every image was CLEANed for 20,000 iterations with loop gain 0.1. The image grid was 1536 by 1536 pixels at 60 arcsec per pixel. Both uvw-space faceting and w-projection used in-memory gridding and Fourier transforms. In this benchmark the maximum w-projection kernel support was 220 pixels full width; each w-plane used a 512 by 512 complex-pixel kernel requiring 2 MB.
- **Dynamic-range definitions:** DR1 is peak brightness divided by the median absolute deviation from the image median. DR2 is peak brightness divided by the absolute value of the strongest nearby negative feature around a bright source. DR1 is a robust global image measure, while DR2 is sensitive to coherent source-local artifacts. The paper does not report RMSE, PSNR, SSIM, or a source-catalog photometric error.
- **Dynamic range and runtime:** Standard two-dimensional Fourier transformation achieved DR1 = 1,570 and DR2 = 2.8, with 27 s per residual calculation and 216 s total deconvolution time. A 9 by 9 uvw-facet run reached DR1 = 12,580 and DR2 = 40, with 1,348 s per residual calculation and 30,488 s total. W-projection with 64 w-planes reached higher DR1 = 18,198 and DR2 = 524, with 83 s of kernel initialization, 607 s per residual calculation, and 3,161 s total. Thus this representative higher-dynamic-range w-projection run took about one tenth the total time of the 9 by 9 facet run. With 256 planes, w-projection reached the table's best DR1 = 50,888 and DR2 = 984, with 231 s initialization, 507 s per residual calculation, and 5,419 s total.
- **Scaling and tradeoffs:** Across 8-256 w-planes, w-projection kernel-initialization time rises from 9 to 231 s and dynamic range improves, while residual-calculation time remains roughly flat at 671-507 s. The authors summarize w-projection as about 20 times slower than an uncorrected two-dimensional transform for this field because its typical 30 by 30 complex convolution region performs about 24 times the inner-loop work of 9 by 9 real gridding. At comparable dynamic range it is about an order of magnitude faster than uvw-space faceting. They report about a factor of 30 advantage in residual-calculation time and about a factor of 3 in error performance over facets, both in favor of w-projection; the overall advantage is smaller because faceting can skip empty facets early in CLEAN and has favorable minor-cycle scaling.
- **Real-data capability demonstration:** A 1.4 GHz image of supernova remnant SN1006 combines all four VLA configurations with Green Bank Telescope data. A maximum-entropy GBT image initializes multi-scale CLEAN, and w-projection with 256 planes performs the major cycle. The paper estimates that an image-plane facet method would need at least 10 by 10 facets. This is a qualitative reconstruction demonstration: it reports no runtime, controlled fidelity metric, noise level, or hardware result for SN1006.
- **Limitations:** Greater convolution support raises memory demand, and the authors state that the worst few-hundred-kilometer, meter-wavelength cases were not feasible on a single-processor machine of the period. Table II presents scaled kernel-support estimates rather than measured end-to-end runtimes for those future-array regimes. A hybrid w-projection and uvw-faceting design is suggested for such cases.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
W-projection is a direct classical reference for the visibility-to-gridding and iFFT stages. It absorbs the non-coplanar w-term into a deterministic, visibility-dependent convolution kernel, then retains a conventional two-dimensional inverse FFT and a major/minor-cycle deconvolution architecture. A learned gridding or reconstruction system intended for wide fields should therefore be compared under the same w-distribution and field geometry, and should report both global and source-local artifact measures rather than only a single image score.

The paper also separates kernel construction, residual calculation, total deconvolution time, memory, and accuracy as the number of w-planes changes. That decomposition is useful for evaluating ML replacements: preprocessing and model-loading costs should not be hidden inside or outside runtime selectively, hardware and image dimensions must be fixed, and fidelity gains should be measured against both an uncorrected Fourier baseline and a geometry-correcting classical baseline. The SN1006 example further shows that the imaging operator can support non-pixel deconvolution, but it does not provide a quantitative real-data benchmark for training or testing an ML method.
