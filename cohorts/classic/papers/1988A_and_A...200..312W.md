# The Multi-Resolution Clean and its application to the short-spacing problem in interferometry

**Bibcode:** 1988A&A...200..312W
**Authors:** B. P. Wakker and U. J. Schwarz
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988A%26A...200..312W/abstract
**Full text:** https://articles.adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1988A%26A...200..312W

## One-line summary
Wakker and Schwarz introduce Multi-Resolution CLEAN (MRC), which applies conventional CLEAN separately to smoothed and difference dirty maps and recombines the results at full resolution, improving extended-source flux recovery, suppression of the negative bowl, and practical speed without recovering spatial frequencies that were never measured.

## Method
MRC starts from the full-resolution dirty map and normalized dirty beam. Both are convolved with a normalized smoothing function whose width makes the smoothed dirty beam's FWHM larger by a factor \(f\). Subtracting the properly rescaled smoothed quantities from the originals produces a difference dirty map and difference dirty beam. Conventional CLEAN is then run independently on the smoothed map, which concentrates extended emission into fewer beam areas and raises its per-pixel signal-to-noise ratio, and on the difference map, which retains small-scale structure while largely excluding the extended negative bowl. The two sets of delta components and residual maps are rescaled and combined with corresponding smoothed and difference clean beams to form a clean map at the original resolution.

The paper derives the scaling needed for the combined reconstruction to remain consistent with the original dirty map and shows that, for noise-free data, its components converge to the same limit as conventional CLEAN. The demonstrated implementation is strictly a two-scale or "Double-Resolution Clean"; a full hierarchy follows by recursively splitting the smoothed map again. The smoothing can instead be applied while making the dirty image by tapering the uv data, with the difference map formed from the difference between two tapers that cancel at zero spacing.

MRC adds a smoothing ratio and an independent second loop gain to the usual CLEAN controls. The authors suggest \(f\) near 2 for relatively small sources and larger values for very extended sources. For low signal-to-noise fields, they define the component-search mask by further smoothing the smooth dirty map, selecting pixels above a signal-to-noise threshold, and repeating masked CLEAN with successively lower cutoffs. This uses no assumed flux model, but it does assume that the sky becomes sparse at some coarser resolution.

The numerical study uses two-dimensional Gaussian source models convolved with a full-synthesis Westerbork Synthesis Radio Telescope dirty beam of 20 arcsec FWHM. Circular model FWHMs are 0, 2, 5, 10, 15, and 20 arcmin, corresponding to 0, 6, 15, 30, 45, and 60 synthesized beams, plus an elongated 5 x 15 arcmin model corresponding to 15 x 45 beams. Peak-to-noise ratios are infinite, 20, and 5, and \(f=4\) throughout. CLEAN uses gain 0.25 and a 1-sigma cutoff; MRC uses gains 0.4 and 0.7 for the smooth and difference maps. Both methods are given a search area restricted to where the simulated signal is expected. Table 1 calculations ran on a VAX 8600 at the University of Groningen.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Recovered integrated flux in arbitrary units:** Table 1 integrates each clean map in rings and annotates whether the cumulative flux profile is flat, still rising, decreasing, or strongly changing. At peak-to-noise ratio 20, the 5 arcmin circular model has true flux 1002; MRC recovers 962 with a flat cumulative profile, while CLEAN recovers 563 with a strongly changing profile. For the 10 arcmin model, MRC recovers 2788 of 4010 with a flat profile, while CLEAN recovers 653 and its profile is strongly changing. For the elongated 5 x 15 arcmin model, MRC recovers 2680 of 3007 with a flat profile. These values are evaluated within the source-dependent radii listed in Table 1, not over a common aperture.
- **Low-signal-to-noise flux recovery:** at peak-to-noise ratio 5, MRC recovers 152 of 160, 1232 of 1002, and 3237 of 4010 arbitrary flux units for the 2, 5, and 10 arcmin circular models. CLEAN recovers 125, 162, and 327, respectively. The MRC over-recovery for the 5 arcmin model shows that recovered flux is not an unbiased error metric in the noisy experiment.
- **Peak brightness and component count:** Table 1 also reports reconstructed peak brightness and the number of delta functions for each method. MRC lists separate component counts for its smoothed and difference-map CLEAN operations. These counts measure computational work and convergence behavior rather than image fidelity by themselves.
- **Realistic-source-model test:** a clean map derived from a real source is reused as a known model, then passed through the simulated dirty-map experiment. The model is about 30 arcmin, or 90 beams, across but appears only about 15 arcmin across in the dirty map. From a true flux of 10,000 arbitrary units, MRC recovers 9358, or 93.6%, using \(16 \times 2822 + 1049 = 46{,}201\) effective delta functions; CLEAN recovers 6910, or 69.1%, after 15,606 delta functions and leaves the negative bowl present. This is a controlled model test, not a benchmark against unknown real-sky truth.
- **Wall-clock time:** the Figure 5 caption reports 90 minutes for CLEAN to find approximately 15,000 components at a 1-sigma cutoff, versus 15 minutes for MRC to find the equivalent of approximately 50,000 components, a 6x timing ratio. The body text says MRC needed "one third" of CLEAN's time, so the paper is internally inconsistent about the ratio. It does not restate the hardware for this timing figure.
- **Analytical operation ratio:** under an in-core, image-domain cost model, the authors count convolutions, masking, decimation, component subtraction, and map recombination. For typical \(f=2\) to 4 and 3 to 6 search-mask iterations, they predict MRC has lower operation count for extended or mixed-scale sources but extra overhead for compact sources. In their peak-to-noise-ratio-20 tests, the reported MRC-to-CLEAN operation ratios are about 0.6 for the 2 arcmin model and about 0.25 for the 5 arcmin model; in the latter case CLEAN recovers only about half as much flux.
- **Qualitative fidelity diagnostics:** map-domain profiles, Fourier-domain profiles, radial cumulative-flux curves, and contour maps assess zero-level flattening, the residual negative bowl, sidelobes, and corrugation. The authors find CLEAN and MRC broadly comparable below 15 beams in source width, although MRC is already superior at 6 beams, and find MRC increasingly useful for larger sources. No standardized RMSE, PSNR, SSIM, residual-visibility norm, uncertainty estimate, or dynamic-range benchmark is reported.
- **Fundamental limitation:** neither MRC nor CLEAN can recover structure whose visibility function lies entirely inside unmeasured short spacings. In the test with a 60-beam circular Gaussian, essentially all Fourier power lies inside the shortest observed spacing and useful recovery fails; an elongated 15 x 45 beam source retains information along its narrower dimension and remains partly recoverable. The algorithm therefore mitigates deconvolution and signal-detection problems but does not replace single-dish data when all relevant short-spacing information is absent.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
MRC is a direct classical baseline for the deconvolution stage after visibility weighting, gridding, and inverse Fourier transformation. Its split into a high-signal-to-noise coarse image and a full-resolution difference image is an early multiscale residual architecture: a learned deconvolver could process scale-specific dirty images or residuals while a physics-based recombination step preserves consistency with the dirty image and PSF.

The paper also provides useful evaluation lessons for an ML replacement. Recovered flux must be measured with a defined aperture and checked for radial convergence; image-domain appearance should be supplemented by Fourier-domain profiles, zero-level and negative-bowl diagnostics, component or iteration cost, and hardware-qualified wall-clock time. Most importantly, the failure on emission confined to unsampled short spacings separates a deconvolution limitation from an information limitation: an ML model may impose a learned prior on that null space, but it cannot claim measured-data recovery without external short-spacing observations or explicit uncertainty.
