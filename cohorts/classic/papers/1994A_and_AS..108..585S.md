# Multi-frequency synthesis techniques in radio interferometric imaging

**Bibcode:** 1994A&AS..108..585S
**Authors:** R. J. Sault and M. H. Wieringa
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1994A%26AS..108..585S/abstract
**Full text:** https://articles.adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1994A%26AS..108..585S

## One-line summary
Sault and Wieringa extend CLEAN to jointly fit source intensity and linear frequency variation from multi-frequency dirty beams, suppressing spectral artifacts while retaining the improved Fourier-plane coverage and sensitivity of wide-band radio interferometric imaging.

## Method
Multi-frequency synthesis grids every visibility at the Fourier coordinate corresponding to its actual observing frequency and inverts all frequencies into one dirty image. This fills radial gaps in the sampled \(u-v\) plane, and simultaneous frequency measurements increase effective sensitivity without conventional bandwidth smearing. The complication is that intrinsic source spectra, the frequency-dependent primary beam, and, for Stokes Q and U, Faraday rotation make apparent sky brightness vary across the band.

The paper models a point source near reference frequency \(\nu_0\) as the sum of two responses: a conventional dirty beam \(B_0\), weighted by reference-frequency intensity, and a spectral dirty beam \(B_1\), weighted by the local frequency derivative or equivalent spectral index. Unlike the earlier alternating "double deconvolution" method, the proposed CLEAN-like iteration estimates the component position and both beam coefficients together. It precomputes beam-beam and dirty-image-beam correlations with FFTs, selects the pixel that maximizes the reduction in squared residual error, solves a two-coefficient least-squares system there, subtracts a loop-gain fraction, and repeats. The implementation uses a typical loop gain of 0.1-0.5 and can be combined with either Hogbom or Clark CLEAN.

The joint fit constrains intensity and spectral components to the same sky location, does not require approximately orthogonal spectral beams, and generalizes to more than two basis beams. Linear and logarithmic expansions cover smooth or power-law spectra; higher-order terms can represent spectral curvature. FFT correlation requires zero padding, a guard band, and cropped beam support to prevent cyclic and edge artifacts. The method is implemented in Miriad and is also used inside iterative self-calibration, where model visibilities account for each channel's frequency-dependent \(u-v\) coordinate and fitted spectral variation.

The demonstration images the FR II radio galaxy PKSB1733-565 with the six-antenna, 6 km east-west Australia Telescope Compact Array. Two 14 h observations used configurations spanning 750 m and 3 km, respectively, and 14 frequency settings from 4.418 to 6.099 GHz. Each setting supplied 32 channels over 128 MHz; the central 25 channels were retained separately rather than averaged. The reduction used AIPS for initial flagging, then Miriad for further flagging, calibration, imaging, about 14 self-calibration iterations, and deconvolution. The final multi-frequency CLEAN result is compared qualitatively with traditional CLEAN applied to the same dirty image and beam.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Image residual noise (off-source RMS, not R2D2 RDR):** blank regions of the final multi-frequency CLEAN image have an rms about 1.5 times the stated 60 microJy beam^-1 thermal-noise limit, or about 90 microJy beam^-1. The paper reports remaining errors around the 535 mJy core and northeast of the northeast hot spot, which the authors could not identify as gain-amplitude or phase errors. It does not report a single measured dynamic-range ratio for the final image.
- **Artifact visibility against traditional CLEAN:** Figure 3 displays both reconstructions saturated at +/-800 microJy beam^-1, equal to +/-0.15% of the peak intensity. Traditional CLEAN uses the same dirty image and beam and benefits from the multi-frequency self-calibration model and flattened core spectrum, but shows substantial spectral artifacts around the northeast hot spot. The comparison is visual rather than a scalar error, residual-rms, or ground-truth fidelity score. The affected hot spot has 1.45 Jy integrated flux density, about three times the southwest hot spot's flux.
- **Spectral-index recovery:** the \(B_0\) and \(B_1\) component images are convolved to 5 arcsec FWHM before division, intentionally coarser than the 1.2 arcsec intensity-image resolution. Apparent spectral indices are approximately -1.05 and -0.90 for the northeast and southwest hot spots. Correcting primary-beam contributions of -0.45 and -0.3, respectively, and the self-calibration flattening gives an intrinsic spectral index of approximately -1.20 for both. The paper cautions that self-calibration can lose the absolute spectral-index scale, so relative indices are only described as moderately reliable unless the scale is calibrated externally.
- **Observation and calibration context:** the target has about 2.5 Jy integrated flux density and a roughly 0.5 Jy core. The two 14 h tracks were recorded on 1992 August 21 and November 5. Frequency settings were switched every five 15 s integrations, with the first integration after each switch discarded because of system-temperature transients. The 14 settings each contained 32 4 MHz channels over 128 MHz; only 25 channels per setting were retained. The core lay about 50 arcsec from the phase center, and averaging even the retained channels at a single setting, with only about 2% fractional bandwidth, would have visibly broadened it through bandwidth smearing.
- **Flux-scale qualification:** calibration used the then-adopted PKSB1934-638 model of 5.71 Jy with spectral index -1.38 at 5.18 GHz. The paper records a preliminary revised model of 5.45 Jy and -1.17; if confirmed, its reported flux densities would need multiplication by 0.95 and intrinsic spectral indices would need +0.21 added. Self-calibration set the target core to 535 mJy at 5.18 GHz with apparent spectral index -0.1.
- **Computational cost and model limits:** the authors estimate the method to be about 2-3 times more computationally expensive than corresponding conventional Hogbom or Clark CLEAN, plus guard-band overhead, but provide no runtime, processor, memory, or other hardware benchmark. They state that higher-order spectral terms become essential for high-dynamic-range imaging when the fractional frequency spread exceeds about 20-30%. Values quoted from Conway et al. rather than newly measured here include less than 1% flux-density bias from the linear approximation and residual errors of order \(5 \times 10^{-4}\) of the second-brightest feature for a +/-12.5% frequency spread after flattening the dominant component's spectrum.
- **No ground-truth benchmark:** evaluation is a real-observation case study. It provides thermal-noise context, image saturation levels, spectral-index estimates, and a same-data qualitative CLEAN comparison, but no synthetic truth image, normalized reconstruction error, PSNR, SSIM, uncertainty interval, formal ablation, or hardware-timed throughput result.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
The paper connects the complete classical wide-band path: channelized visibilities are calibrated, each sample is gridded at its true frequency-dependent \(u-v\) coordinate, a joint dirty image and multiple spectral dirty beams are formed by FFT, and deconvolution jointly estimates spatial intensity and spectral coefficients. Its matched-filter correlations, local least-squares coefficient solve, residual update, loop gain, and stopping threshold are natural components for unrolling or learned replacement, while the co-located intensity and spectral components provide an explicit structured prior.

For ML evaluation, the paper identifies fidelity dimensions that a single image-domain score would miss: spectral artifacts around bright components, thermal-noise proximity, primary-beam-induced apparent spectra, absolute spectral-scale loss during self-calibration, bandwidth smearing from premature channel averaging, and sensitivity to spectral curvature. The traditional-CLEAN comparison is a useful historical baseline but lacks ground truth and compute measurements, so a modern visibility-to-image study should preserve the paper's frequency, calibration, beam, flux, and resolution context while adding reproducible residual, reconstruction, spectral-index, runtime, and hardware metrics.
