# Image Domain Gridding: a fast method for convolutional resampling of visibilities

**Bibcode:** 2018A&A...616A..27V
**Authors:** Sebastiaan van der Tol, Bram Veenboer, André R. Offringa
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2018A%26A...616A..27V/abstract
**Full text:** https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2018/08/aa32858-18.pdf
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/201832858

## One-line summary
Image Domain Gridding (IDG) replaces oversampled AW-projection convolution kernels with small image-domain grids for groups of nearby visibilities, enabling time- and direction-dependent corrections to map efficiently to GPUs while matching or improving the throughput and numerical accuracy of classical gridding in the paper's tests.

## Method
IDG partitions visibilities into blocks whose convolution support fits within a small subgrid of the master uv grid. For each block, it directly accumulates a low-resolution image from the visibilities, including the phase shift from the subgrid center and the image-domain taper or correction term. A small FFT converts this image to a uv subgrid, which is shifted and added to the master grid. Degridding reverses these operations. The method therefore avoids computing, storing, and looking up oversampled convolution kernels. Direction-, time-, baseline-, and frequency-dependent effects can be evaluated directly in the small image domain rather than encoded in repeatedly recomputed AW-projection kernels.

The paper derives an optimal separable taper by minimizing the mean-square sidelobe energy of the effective image-domain window over all allowed sample positions in a subgrid. This becomes an eigenvalue problem, with the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue providing the taper. The implementation is exercised through a modified WSClean. Its simulation uses a 48 x 48 pixel IDG subgrid; the classical baseline uses WSClean's Kaiser-Bessel kernel of width 7 with oversampling factor 63.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Analytic aliasing error:** the normalized energy in the sidelobes of the effective window is used to compare the optimal IDG taper against classical gridding with a prolate spheroidal wave function. Table 1 shows that accuracy depends on both kernel main-lobe width beta and IDG subgrid size L. For example, at beta = 7, the reported error is 7.96e-5 for classical gridding and 6.62e-5 for IDG with L = 64; at beta = 3, it is 3.33e-2 and 1.32e-2, respectively. Smaller IDG subgrids can be worse, so the result is not an unconditional advantage at every configuration.
- **Simulation setup and baselines:** tests use the metadata of an 8.5 h LOFAR Toothbrush-cluster observation: 20 channels over 130-132 MHz, 55 stations, baselines from 1 to 84 km, 10 s integrations, 3,122 timesteps, and 4,636,170 rows, yielding about 93 million channel-visibilities. A 1 Jy point source is placed in a 2048 x 2048 image with 1 arcsec cells. Visibilities are generated by direct measurement-equation evaluation, classical WSClean degridding, and IDG.
- **Visibility-domain RMS error against direct evaluation:** on the 1 km baseline, IDG reports 1.03e-5 Jy versus 2.51e-3 Jy for classical gridding, about 242 times lower. On the 84 km baseline, IDG reports 7.10e-4 Jy versus 4.78e-3 Jy, about 7 times lower.
- **Residual-image RMS:** in the box around the simulated source, IDG reports 7.6e-6 Jy/beam versus 1.3e-4 Jy/beam for classical gridding, described as about 19 times lower. Across the entire residual image, the values are 1.1e-6 and 2.1e-5 Jy/beam, described as about 17 times lower. The text summarizes the residual-noise reduction as roughly a factor of 18 and notes that this improves the dynamic-range limit only when gridding error is the limiting factor.
- **PSF sidelobes:** the RMS over the PSF excluding its main lobe is about 5% lower for IDG than for classical gridding. The authors expect this difference to have little noticeable effect on deconvolution convergence or total runtime.
- **Out-of-field-source suppression:** the dirty-image RMS for a simulated source outside the imaged area is reported as 2% lower with IDG. Figure 8's caption prints 1.35 Jy/beam for IDG and 1.38e-3 Jy/beam for classical gridding, which is internally inconsistent with both the stated 2% difference and the plotted scale. The percentage is therefore reportable, but the first absolute value should not be treated as reliable without an erratum.
- **Throughput and runtime:** on an NVIDIA GeForce 840M laptop GPU with a dual-core, hyperthreaded 2.60 GHz Intel i7 host, the low-level IDG routine processes 4.3 million visibilities/s, taking 22 s for about 93 million visibilities. WSClean reports 72 s including large FFTs, I/O, and other overhead. The classical-gridding run takes 52 s for Stokes I and 192 s for all four polarizations; IDG always computes all four polarizations, so the single-Stokes timing is not a like-for-like baseline.
- **Observed-data scaling run:** the LOFAR job runs on a node with two 8-core Intel Xeon E5-2630 v3 CPUs at 2.40 GHz, 32 hardware threads total, and four NVIDIA Tesla K40c GPUs rated at 4.29 Tflop/s single precision each. Briggs weighting with robustness 0 and a 100 mJy cleaning threshold requires four major cycles of about 20 min each. This run measures runtime scaling, not image fidelity: the direction-dependent corrections are identity matrices. The section text describes a 30,000 x 30,000 image at 1.2 arcsec/pixel, clipped to 24,000 x 24,000 or 8 x 8 degrees, while Figure 9 instead captions a 20,000 x 20,000 image at 4 arcsec/pixel over 22 x 22 degrees; these incompatible descriptions should not be silently combined.
- **Scope limitation:** the paper does not test actual time- or direction-dependent correction quality. Although the runtime includes the correction path, the correction matrices are identities, so the results establish gridding accuracy and computational feasibility rather than calibrated wide-field fidelity.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
IDG directly targets the visibility-to-grid stage: it turns irregular visibilities into FFT-ready uv subgrids while applying measurement-equation effects in small image-domain tiles. It is therefore a strong non-ML gridding baseline for any learned replacement, with metrics that isolate aliasing error, visibility RMS against direct evaluation, residual-image RMS, PSF sidelobes, out-of-field leakage, throughput, and end-to-end gridding time. Its small, regular FFTs and highly parallel per-block accumulation also provide a GPU-friendly differentiable operator that could sit before an iFFT and learned deconvolver, while the identity-correction limitation means a later comparison should separately test real direction-dependent corrections.
