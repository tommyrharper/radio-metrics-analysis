# Scalable precision wide-field imaging in radio interferometry: I. uSARA validated on ASKAP data

**Bibcode:** 2023MNRAS.522.5558W
**Authors:** Amanda G. Wilber, Arwa Dabbech, Adrian Jackson, Yves Wiaux
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.5558W/abstract
**Full text:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.14148
**arXiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14148
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stad1351

## One-line summary
uSARA combines a parallel wide-field measurement operator with non-negative, reweighted average-sparsity regularization to reconstruct super-resolved compact and diffuse structure from real ASKAP visibilities; compared with multi-scale WSClean, it produces more detailed 2.2 arcsec-pixel images and spectral-index maps but costs roughly an order of magnitude more computation and can suppress the faintest sources when regularization is too strong.

## Method
The paper validates the unconstrained Sparsity Averaging Reweighted Analysis (uSARA) algorithm on three imperfectly calibrated ASKAP beam observations. The linear measurement model includes Gaussian visibility noise, Fourier sampling through a non-uniform FFT, Briggs data weighting, and a hybrid correction of the non-coplanar baseline effect through w-stacking and w-projection. Noise whitening is incorporated into the data and measurement operator. The operator is partitioned by w-stack, then further distributed into memory-constrained blocks of radially neighboring Fourier samples. An automated planning step selects the number of w-stacks and compute resources. When direct de-gridding matrices are too large, visibility gridding forms sparse holographic matrices for the normal operator, making its memory requirement depend mainly on image size.

uSARA minimizes a Gaussian squared-l2 data-fidelity term plus a non-convex log-sum regularizer that promotes average sparsity across the normalized concatenation of nine orthogonal bases, subject to image non-negativity. Iterative reweighting replaces the log-sum term by a sequence of weighted l1 convex problems. Each problem is approximately solved by forward-backward iterations: a gradient step applies the normal measurement operator, and a proximal denoising step performs sub-iterative soft thresholding in the sparsity dictionary. The dictionary and denoising computation are spatially faceted across CPU cores. The step size is fixed to 1.98/L, where L is the gradient Lipschitz constant, and the regularization strength is derived from the estimated image-domain noise and the Briggs-weighting correction. For the ASKAP sub-band experiments, the authors reduce this heuristic regularization strength to 0.7-0.8 of its nominal value; the full-band experiment uses the heuristic directly. Runs stop after at most 10 reweighted minimization tasks or at relative image variation 0.0005.

The experiments use public ASKAP Early Science and EMU Pilot observations: SB8275 beam 15 spans 870-1158 MHz, while SB9351 beam 12 and SB9442 beam 35 span 800-1088 MHz. Each observation has 10 h integration and 288 MHz instantaneous bandwidth. The data are split into eight 36 MHz sub-bands of about 0.8-1.2 GB each; SB9442 is also reconstructed as one 7.5 GB full-band monochromatic dataset. Both uSARA and WSClean use baselines longer than 60 m, Briggs robust -0.25 weighting, 2.2 arcsec pixels, and matching fields of view. The first two fields use 5500 x 5500 images over 3.36 degrees, and SB9442 uses 4096 x 4096 images over 2.5 degrees. The 2.2 arcsec pixel size is described as a super-resolution factor of two at the highest frequency relative to ASKAP's 9-12 arcsec native instrumental resolution.

The benchmark is multi-scale CLEAN in WSClean with loop gain 0.8, automatic masks down to 2.5 times the residual standard deviation, a stopping threshold equal to the residual standard deviation, and at most 1,000,000 iterations. WSClean outputs restored images, formed from its model convolved with the restoring beam plus the residual; the paper also evaluates beam-smoothed WSClean model images to separate model flux from residual noise. uSARA outputs unconvolved model images at the 2.2 arcsec pixel scale. The comparison therefore tests scientifically usable products but is not an equal-resolution, ground-truth reconstruction benchmark.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Evaluation scope and photometric caveats:** The paper evaluates source morphology, integrated diffuse flux density, in-band spectral-index maps, residual-map rms, recovered contour levels, and computation. Flux is integrated within manually drawn source regions that approximately follow the 2-sigma contour in WSClean, using identical regions between algorithms for a given source and sub-band. WSClean restored images are normalized from Jy/beam to Jy/pixel by the restoring-beam area. No primary-beam correction is applied. The ASKAPsoft IAU Stokes-I convention is not corrected by the usual factor of two, so reported image fluxes are half those in ASKAPsoft mosaics. No flux-density uncertainties are reported because the Early Science and Pilot data had not been validated against standard flux catalogues.
- **Resolution and morphology, measured context plus qualitative assessment:** uSARA models retain a 2.2 x 2.2 arcsec pixel scale, while the first-sub-band WSClean restoring beams are 9.4 x 10.9 arcsec for SB8275 and 10.1 x 16.4 arcsec for SB9351; the SB9442 full-band restoring beam is 11.8 x 9.4 arcsec. The authors qualitatively find sharper jets, lobes, filaments, diffuse-source boundaries, and separation of blended structures in uSARA. These are visual comparisons on real data, not effective-resolution estimates against known truth. uSARA also misses some of the faintest point sources and does not clearly recover the SPT2023 halo, which the authors attribute to the selected regularization strength.
- **Abell 3395 phoenix diffuse-flux comparison:** Across the eight sub-bands centered at 887-1139 MHz, uSARA measures 29.3, 24.3, 14.5, 10.4, 13.5, 15.2, 7.3, and 7.4 mJy. WSClean restored images measure 30.2, 25.1, 25.2, 20.0, 25.7, 21.6, 10.0, and 8.3 mJy, while its smoothed models measure 27.2, 22.2, 23.8, 18.6, 23.4, 19.7, 9.5, and 7.1 mJy (Table 3). In the 887 MHz image, the uSARA source boundary follows about 1 microJy/pixel even though its calculated image-noise standard deviation is 6 microJy/pixel; the WSClean boundary is clearer at 2-3 times its measured 2 microJy/pixel residual rms. The lower restored-image flux agreement at higher frequencies is interpreted as residual-noise contamination and possibly a bias from monochromatic rather than wide-band cleaning, not established by a truth-based error metric.
- **PKS 2014-55 X-shaped galaxy flux and sensitivity:** From 817 to 1069 MHz, uSARA integrated flux falls from 758.8 to 268.2 mJy, WSClean restored-image flux from 756.4 to 352.1 mJy, and WSClean smoothed-model flux from 743.7 to 308.4 mJy (Table 4). Except in the first sub-band, WSClean's restored-image flux is higher; smoothing its model brings the values closer to uSARA, consistent with the paper's residual-noise explanation. At 817 MHz, the uSARA source border is about 1 microJy/pixel against a calculated 6 microJy/pixel image-noise standard deviation, whereas WSClean's border is clearer at 3 times its measured residual rms of 1.6 microJy/pixel. Because the products have different effective resolution and no truth image, these contour levels are evidence of recovered low-surface-brightness structure rather than a controlled detection-limit measurement.
- **SPT2023 relic and missed halo:** Across 817-1069 MHz, uSARA measures relic fluxes from 4.9 down to 1.3 mJy, compared with 5.7 down to 3.4 mJy in WSClean restored images and 5.0 down to 2.7 mJy in smoothed WSClean models (Table 5). The uSARA image renders the relic more clearly but does not recover the much fainter radio halo. The paper notes that the prior halo classification used a more sensitive 288 MHz multi-frequency-synthesis image, whereas this comparison uses 36 MHz monochromatic sub-bands.
- **PKS 2130-538 Dancing Ghosts full-band and sub-band flux:** On the comparatively clean SB9442 field, the 943 MHz full-band integrated flux is 117.2 mJy for uSARA and 115.5 mJy for both the WSClean restored image and smoothed model. Across 817-1069 MHz, uSARA falls from 131.0 to 101.8 mJy, WSClean restored images from 128.8 to 101.0 mJy, and WSClean smoothed models from 128.6 to 101.0 mJy (Table 6). This close agreement contrasts with the steeper, fainter sources in the calibration-artefact-dominated fields. Both full-band products recover the source boundary at 3 microJy/pixel. This is 3 times WSClean's measured 1 microJy/pixel residual rms but below uSARA's calculated 8 microJy/pixel image-domain noise standard deviation.
- **Spectral-index fidelity:** Spectral-index maps fit a first-order polynomial to log flux versus log frequency using the first six sub-bands. uSARA images are smoothed to a common 5 arcsec circular beam, while WSClean images are smoothed to 20 arcsec. Both algorithms show broadly consistent steepening and flattening patterns, while uSARA maps contain more small-scale detail. Reported examples include an Abell 3395 phoenix core near spectral index 1.5 surrounded by emission below 1, Dancing Ghosts lobes mostly between 1 and 2, and a north-east filament that changes from 2.9 down to 0.9 and back up to 3.2. The authors explicitly call this spectral analysis preliminary: higher-frequency diffuse structure can disappear, causing artificial steepening, and the unequal smoothing prevents an equal-resolution accuracy comparison. No spectral-index error, correlation coefficient, or ground-truth RMSE is reported.
- **Compute, memory, and scale on Cirrus:** All runs use the Cirrus UK Tier-2 system, whose nodes have 36 physical CPU cores, 72 hardware threads, and 256 GB memory. The largest uSARA run processes 467 million visibilities from the 7.5 GB SB9442 full-band dataset using 19 w-stacks and 64 image facets. Holographic encoding reduces the normal-operator storage to 80 GB, described as nearly a factor-of-five reduction relative to de-gridding matrices. Operator precomputation costs 281 CPU core-hours, and deconvolution costs 770 CPU core-hours or 4.3 h wall time. WSClean processes the same full-band field in 58 CPU core-hours or 0.8 h on one node. Across sub-bands, uSARA takes 5.4-6.9 h for SB8275, 8.1-8.8 h for SB9351, and 5.2-5.9 h for SB9442; WSClean takes 0.2-0.7 h. The paper characterizes uSARA's current MATLAB prototype as about 20 times more costly on average and WSClean as roughly an order of magnitude faster, while expecting a C++ parallel implementation to narrow the gap.
- **Metric limitations:** The real-data study reports no PSNR, SSIM, normalized RMSE, source completeness, calibrated dynamic-range number, or visibility-residual statistic against known truth. Calibration artefacts extend up to degrees from bright sources in two fields, no direction-dependent recalibration is performed, and the regularization parameter is manually reduced from its noise-based heuristic for sub-band image quality. Claims of better fidelity and previously unseen structure are therefore supported by morphology, flux consistency, spectral trends, and residual-noise context, but not by a controlled truth-based benchmark.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
uSARA is directly relevant as a non-learned precision alternative for the deconvolution stage and as the optimization backbone later reused by AIRI with a learned denoiser. Its forward step repeatedly applies a noise-whitened wide-field visibility operator, including NUFFT sampling, weighting, and w-effect correction, rather than treating one dirty image as a complete sufficient input. Its backward step is a handcrafted multi-basis denoiser, making it a useful matched baseline for testing whether an ML denoiser adds fidelity or speed without changing data handling, stopping criteria, or the surrounding forward-backward solver.

The paper also identifies evaluation requirements for an end-to-end visibility-to-image ML system. Comparisons should preserve visibility count and volume, uv selection, Briggs weighting, field and pixel size, primary-beam and calibration treatment, image resolution, flux units, masking, and hardware. They should separately measure model flux, residual contamination, effective angular resolution, diffuse-source completeness, spectral-index error, and visibility-domain consistency. In particular, low contour levels relative to an analytically estimated image-noise level are not substitutes for injection-based completeness or ground-truth error, and a learned method should be tested on direction-dependent calibration errors and faint sources that a stronger prior may suppress.
