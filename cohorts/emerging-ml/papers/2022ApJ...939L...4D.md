# First AI for Deep Super-resolution Wide-field Imaging in Radio Astronomy: Unveiling Structure in ESO 137-006

**Bibcode:** 2022ApJ...939L...4D
**Authors:** Arwa Dabbech, Matthieu Terris, Adrian Jackson, Mpati A. Ramatsoku, Oleg M. Smirnov, Yves Wiaux
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...939L...4D/abstract
**Full text:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.11336
**arXiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.11336
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/ac98af

## One-line summary
The paper applies AIRI, a plug-and-play forward-backward imager whose learned DnCNN denoiser regularizes an explicit wide-field visibility measurement model, to two MeerKAT continuum bands totaling about 19 GB and reports deeper, more highly resolved structure than multi-scale CLEAN, similar AGN flux recovery to uSARA and CLEAN, and about 2.3 times lower imaging CPU cost than uSARA, while providing no truth-based image-fidelity benchmark.

## Method
The framework reconstructs a monochromatic Stokes-I intensity image from complex visibilities under a Gaussian-noise measurement model. Its forward-backward iterations alternate a gradient step on the squared-l2 visibility data-fidelity term with an image-denoising step. The gradient uses the explicit measurement operator and its adjoint, rather than only a dirty image or point-spread-function approximation. For the 1.91 degree field, the operator combines non-uniform Fourier interpolation with a hybrid w-stacking and w-projection treatment of non-coplanar baselines. It is partitioned into w-stacks and radial Fourier-plane blocks, and can replace separate de-gridding and gridding matrices by sparse holographic normal-operator matrices when memory requires it.

The paper compares two regularizers in the same optimization structure. uSARA uses a handcrafted, sub-iterative proximal denoiser that promotes image non-negativity and reweighted log-sum sparsity in a normalized concatenation of orthogonal wavelet bases. AIRI replaces it with a convolutional DnCNN denoiser trained on normalized synthetic images corrupted with Gaussian noise. The training objective combines an l1 denoising loss with a relaxed penalty encouraging firm non-expansiveness, which supports convergence of the plug-and-play iterations. The denoiser sees neither the visibility sampling pattern nor the telescope configuration, and the authors adapt a single trained denoiser to each observation by rescaling the inverse problem according to estimated image-domain noise and an upper bound on peak intensity. The denoiser used for both bands was trained for a target dynamic range of 4 x 10^5 and noise standard deviation 2.5 x 10^-6 Jy.

The experiment uses about 7 hours of MeerKAT L-band observations of ESO 137-006 after CARACAL reference calibration, channel averaging, and phase self-calibration with WSClean and CubiCal. Attempts at amplitude and direction-dependent calibration did not materially improve the data, so the reported images retain calibration limitations. Two relatively interference-free bands are imaged independently: 961-1145 MHz centered at 1053 MHz, containing about 532 million double-precision data points in 8.2 GB, and 1295-1503 MHz centered at 1399 MHz, containing about 673 million points in 10.76 GB. All reconstructions use Briggs robust 0 weighting. Images have 4096 x 4096 pixels, span 1.91 x 1.91 degrees, and use 1.68 arcsec cells, corresponding to configured super-resolution factors beyond about 2 at the low band and 1.6 at the high band.

The real-data baselines are uSARA and multi-scale CLEAN in WSClean. uSARA and AIRI produce unconvolved models in Jy/pixel. For comparable displayed intensity units, CLEAN's restored and restoring-beam-convolved model images are normalized by restoring-beam flux. This remains an unequal-product comparison: CLEAN resolution is limited by its restoring beam, while AIRI and uSARA models are presented directly at the super-resolved pixel scale.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Evaluation scale and resolution setting:** The two independently imaged datasets total about 19 GB and 1.205 billion double-precision points. The paper uses the 1.68 arcsec cell size and configured super-resolution factors beyond about 2 and 1.6 as resolution context. It does not estimate effective angular resolution from injected sources or known truth.
- **Estimated noise and target dynamic range, used for parameter selection:** The estimated image-domain noise levels are about 0.0014 mJy at 1053 MHz and 0.0017 mJy at 1399 MHz. Normalized dirty-image peaks of 0.69 and 0.37 Jy imply nominal target dynamic ranges of 5 x 10^5 and 2.2 x 10^5, respectively. The authors caution that these dirty peaks overestimate the reconstructed peaks, which are around 0.05 Jy in both bands, by more than a factor of 10. These are regularization-setting estimates, not measured reconstruction-fidelity scores.
- **AGN flux and spectral-index cross-check:** At 1053 MHz, uSARA, AIRI, and CLEAN recover 143, 143, and 146 mJy, respectively. At 1399 MHz they recover 124, 125, and 123 mJy. The corresponding two-band spectral indices are 0.50, 0.47, and 0.60. CLEAN flux is measured from its convolved model within a region the size of the dirty-beam main lobe, whereas the super-resolved uSARA and AIRI fluxes are summed over active pixels. Agreement is therefore a real-data consistency check, not error against known flux truth.
- **Recovered filament intensity and artifact context:** Candidate inner filaments T1, T2, T3, and the previously reported T0 have per-pixel intensities in the 0.1-0.4 mJy range at the low band, compared with the approximately 0.0014 mJy estimated image-domain noise and levels at least five times lower for identified amplitude-calibration artifacts. T1 and T2 appear in both bands with AIRI and uSARA; CLEAN clearly resolves T2 only at high band, although emission is present at the relevant locations. T3 appears only at low band and is explicitly treated as inconclusive.
- **Qualitative morphology and depth:** AIRI and uSARA show more filamentary lobe detail, a more continuous core-to-jet connection, sharper compact sources, and lower recovered intensities than the CLEAN convolved model. AIRI visually recovers faint compact sources not present in the CLEAN model but present in the CLEAN residual/restored image, which the paper uses as a hallucination cross-check. AIRI also appears deeper than uSARA and less affected by some ringing. These are visual assessments on calibration-limited real data, not scalar accuracy measurements. Super-resolved pixelation is more noticeable in AIRI, and structures labelled S are identified as likely residual amplitude-calibration artifacts because they track dirty-beam geometry.
- **Measurement-operator memory:** Encoding the normal operator with holographic matrices reduces reported storage from 470 to 81 GB at 1053 MHz and from 645 to 159 GB at 1399 MHz. The low- and high-band operators use 12 and 14 w-stacks, respectively; multi-scale WSClean uses 72 at both bands.
- **Computation on Cirrus:** For the low band, sparse measurement-operator construction costs 427 CPU h for either uSARA or AIRI; imaging costs 1120 CPU h for uSARA, 480 CPU h plus 5 GPU h for AIRI, and 132 CPU h for CLEAN. At the high band, construction costs 652 CPU h; imaging costs 2377 CPU h for uSARA, 1028 CPU h plus 6 GPU h for AIRI, and 236 CPU h for CLEAN. Thus AIRI imaging uses about 2.3 times fewer CPU hours than uSARA, but about 4 times the imaging compute of WSClean; including operator construction makes AIRI about 7 times as costly as WSClean. The paper excludes the reusable denoiser's training cost and reports CPU/GPU hours rather than wall time.
- **Parallel hardware context:** On the Cirrus UK Tier-2 system, measurement-operator construction uses 240 CPUs at low band and 280 at high band. Forward steps use 99 and 180 CPUs. uSARA's 8 x 8 denoiser faceting uses 64 of those CPUs; AIRI uses 2 x 2 faceting processed sequentially on one GPU; WSClean uses 72 CPUs. The paper does not name the CPU or GPU models.
- **Missing controlled metrics:** The study reports no PSNR, SSIM, RMSE, visibility-residual statistic, source completeness, false-positive rate, calibrated dynamic-range measurement, or known-truth flux error. Its numerical fidelity evidence is limited chiefly to estimated noise, configured scale, AGN photometry, spectral index, and signal/artifact intensity context. Claims of increased resolution and depth rely substantially on qualitative comparison of differently restored products.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
AIRI is directly relevant as a learned deconvolution regularizer embedded inside an optimization loop that preserves the physical visibility forward model. Every forward step applies a wide-field normal operator encompassing weighting, non-uniform Fourier gridding and de-gridding, FFTs, and w-effect correction; every backward step applies the learned image denoiser. This separates the learned prior from telescope sampling and makes one denoiser reusable across measurement configurations after noise-aware rescaling, while retaining a visibility-domain data-consistency path.

The paper also exposes key systems and evaluation requirements for such a pipeline. Holographic gridding and operator partitioning can make repeated learned deconvolution feasible at billion-visibility scale, but operator construction remains a large cost that should be reported separately from network inference and training. Future comparisons should hold visibility selection, weighting, pixel scale, restoration, and calibration treatment fixed, and should add truth-based effective resolution, injected-source completeness, false detections, flux error, visibility residuals, and wall time on named hardware. The paper's pixelation and calibration-tracking structures also show why agreement across frequencies and algorithms is useful but insufficient as a hallucination or fidelity test.
