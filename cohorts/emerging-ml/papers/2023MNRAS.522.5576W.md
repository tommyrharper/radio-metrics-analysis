# Scalable precision wide-field imaging in radio interferometry: II. AIRI validated on ASKAP data

**Bibcode:** 2023MNRAS.522.5576W
**Authors:** Amanda G. Wilber, Arwa Dabbech, Matthieu Terris, Adrian Jackson, Yves Wiaux
**ADS:** https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2023MNRAS.522.5576W/abstract
**Full text:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.14149
**arXiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14149
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stad1353

## One-line summary
AIRI replaces uSARA's iterative handcrafted sparsity proximal operator with a pre-trained DnCNN denoiser inside the same visibility-domain forward-backward reconstruction framework; on three real ASKAP fields it recovers more diffuse emission and reduces average deconvolution cost by a factor of four relative to uSARA, but its compact detail can be smoother and the real-data study has no ground-truth image-fidelity benchmark.

## Method
The paper validates AI for Regularisation in radio-interferometric Imaging (AIRI) on the same imperfectly calibrated ASKAP data and in the same parallel wide-field framework used for the companion uSARA study. The measurement model maps a non-negative sky image to complex visibilities through a Fourier-sampling operator that includes the non-coplanar-baseline w-effect. Noise whitening and imaging weights such as Briggs weighting are incorporated into the operator. AIRI alternates a forward gradient step on the squared-l2 visibility residual with a backward image-regularisation step. Unlike uSARA, whose backward step approximately evaluates a handcrafted sparsity proximal operator through sub-iterations, AIRI uses one learned denoising convolutional neural network.

The denoisers use a DnCNN architecture trained under supervision to remove zero-mean Gaussian noise from a high-dynamic-range database synthesized from optical astronomy images. Training images are normalized to peak value 1, and the loss includes a non-expansiveness penalty intended to preserve plug-and-play algorithm convergence and solution interpretability. The network training noise standard deviation is interpreted as the inverse target image dynamic range. For a dataset, the authors estimate image-domain noise from the visibility-noise level, the spectral norm of the normal operator, and a weighting correction. Because the true sky peak is unavailable, they use the dirty-image peak as an upper bound and rescale the inverse problem to match a pre-trained denoiser.

Two denoiser-selection strategies are tested. The primary shelf strategy selects the closest denoiser noise level below the estimated inverse target dynamic range from `2e-5`, `4e-5`, and `8e-5`. The universal strategy always uses the highest-dynamic-range denoiser, with noise level `2e-5`, after rescaling. The universal experiment is performed on SB9351-12 spectral windows 2-8; window 1 already selects `2e-5` under the shelf strategy. The authors caution that enforcing the non-expansiveness constraint during training is difficult: at the real-data image sizes and dynamic ranges studied, some trained denoisers were unstable and required further training.

The experiments use three public ASKAP Early Science and Evolutionary Map of the Universe Pilot observations: SB8275-15, SB9351-12, and SB9442-35. Each 288 MHz observation, within an overall 800-1158 MHz range, is divided into eight 36 MHz spectral windows. SB8275-15 and SB9351-12 are reconstructed at 5500 x 5500 pixels over 3.36 degrees, while SB9442-35 uses 4096 x 4096 pixels over about 2.5 degrees. All use 2.2 arcsec pixels. SB9442-35 is additionally reconstructed as a monochromatic 288 MHz full-band image centered at 943 MHz. Each denoiser application is split into four overlapping image facets, one per GPU, to fit GPU memory without visible facet artefacts.

Evaluation reuses the companion paper's uSARA and multi-scale WSClean results. It compares source morphology, integrated flux density, spectral-index maps, denoiser-instance variation, and compute cost. AIRI fluxes are integrated in hand-drawn regions adjusted to AIRI's recovered morphology, so they are not measured in exactly the same regions as the uSARA and WSClean fluxes. Spectral-index maps fit `S_nu proportional to nu^-alpha` using only the first six sub-bands, after smoothing AIRI images with a common 5 arcsec circular Gaussian beam.

## Performance / fidelity metrics used
- **Evaluation scope and limitations:** This is a real-data validation without known sky truth. The paper reports morphology, source-region integrated flux in mJy, spectral-index structure, pixelwise reconstruction variation relative to estimated image noise, and computation. It does not report PSNR, SSIM, RMSE, visibility-residual statistics, source completeness, false detections, an effective angular-resolution measurement, or a calibrated dynamic-range benchmark. The authors state that the early ASKAP observations have not been validated against standard flux catalogues, report no flux-density uncertainties, and caution that separately reconstructed monochromatic sub-bands cannot establish which method's photometry is most reliable.
- **Resolution and morphology, quantitative settings plus qualitative assessment:** AIRI images have 2.2 x 2.2 arcsec pixels, but the paper does not measure their effective point-spread function. Relative to uSARA, AIRI generally reconstructs more extended, smoother diffuse emission, including the Abell 3395 phoenix, the edges of PKS 2014-55, and an east-west SPT2023 halo-like structure most visible in the first two 36 MHz windows. It can lose compact detail within lobes, diminish faint point-source intensities, and make bright-source calibration rings fainter but more extended. These are visual comparisons, not truth-based detection or resolution measurements.
- **Abell 3395 phoenix flux and spectral structure:** At spectral-window centers 887, 923, 959, 995, 1031, 1067, 1103, and 1139 MHz, AIRI integrated fluxes are 25.3, 27.0, 17.9, 8.5, 11.3, 12.1, 9.7, and 1.6 mJy, respectively (Table 1). The paper describes modest variation relative to uSARA and WSClean except for clearly lower AIRI flux in the highest-frequency window. AIRI recovers emission above 1 microJy/pixel over a larger morphology, and its first-six-window spectral-index map places the dim core at alpha 2.1-2.8. The classification interpretation remains qualitative and depends on sub-band recovery.
- **PKS 2014-55 X-shaped galaxy:** AIRI integrated fluxes from 817 through 1069 MHz are 678.7, 580.0, 485.0, 427.7, 436.2, 352.7, 302.3, and 190.5 mJy (Table 2). The regions sum the east wing, west wing, and core. Relative to uSARA, the source has more diffuse extent but less resolved compact lobe structure. AIRI's spectral-index coverage is broader: the south-east leg of the east wing is alpha 1.4-2.1, the west wing averages about alpha 3, and its far north-west portion reaches alpha 3.5-5. The authors explicitly say wide-band deconvolution is required to confirm the ultra-steep values.
- **SPT2023 relic and halo:** AIRI relic fluxes at 817-1069 MHz are 4.3, 3.2, 1.8, 2.0, 2.8, 2.3, 1.7, and 0.5 mJy (Table 3). They are slightly above uSARA only in windows 6 and 7 and below it elsewhere. AIRI visually recovers an east-west halo-like diffuse component most clearly in the first two 36 MHz windows, whereas the companion uSARA image did not; the paper does not provide integrated halo flux, detection significance, or an injection-based completeness test.
- **PKS 2130-538 Dancing Ghosts:** The 943 MHz full-band AIRI flux is 116.7 mJy. Sub-band values at 817-1069 MHz are 128.3, 123.8, 118.1, 113.2, 109.2, 105.7, 102.6, and 97.7 mJy (Table 4). The authors find the closest flux agreement among AIRI, uSARA, and WSClean for this relatively flat-spectrum source in the field with fewer strong calibration artefacts. AIRI's full-band image is sharper than its sub-band result, while an eastern filament is slightly fainter and more diffuse than in uSARA and has reported alpha 3.5-6; no spectral-index uncertainty is given.
- **Denoiser-instance variation and internal reporting inconsistency:** For SB9351-12 windows 2-8, Table 5 reports that 99.6%, 99.7%, 99.7%, 99.3%, 99.5%, 99.2%, and 98.9% of pixels in the shelf-versus-universal absolute-difference maps lie below estimated image noise. Those values imply 0.4%, 0.3%, 0.3%, 0.7%, 0.5%, 0.8%, and 1.1% above noise. The surrounding text instead states that every window has 0.8-1.1% above noise, while the conclusion summarizes the discrepancy as about 1%. Error maps are hard-thresholded below `1e-8` before analysis. For window 7, 2.5% of pixels differ from uSARA by more than estimated noise. This measures sensitivity to denoiser choice, not error against sky truth, and its dependence on the estimated noise threshold should be preserved.
- **Compute and hardware:** AIRI runs on Cirrus GPU nodes, each with four GPUs, 40 CPU cores, and 384 GB shared memory; experiments use one to five nodes according to memory need. CPU cores apply the measurement operator and four GPUs apply the four denoiser facets. SB8275 sub-bands cost 48-144 CPU core-hours and 1.0-2.0 h wall time; SB9351 costs 91-125 CPU core-hours and 2.0-2.9 h; SB9442 sub-bands cost 45-69 CPU core-hours and 1.4-1.6 h. The SB9442 full-band reconstruction costs 203 CPU core-hours and 1.1 h (Table 6). GPU model and training cost are not reported.
- **Compute comparisons and caveats:** The DNN backward step is reported as 10-30 times faster than uSARA's sub-iterative denoiser depending on image size. Total AIRI deconvolution cost is on average four times lower than uSARA and five times higher than WSClean. The comparison is not hardware matched: uSARA ran on Cirrus CPU nodes, AIRI on newer mixed CPU/GPU nodes, and AIRI's forward step alone was up to 1.2 times faster, which the authors attribute to the newer processors. A preliminary test moving forward-step Fourier transforms to GPUs nearly halved total AIRI cost, but this is not one of the tabulated production experiments.

## Relevance to visibility→gridding→iFFT→deconvolution ML pipeline
AIRI is directly relevant to learned deconvolution without replacing the physical visibility model. Its learned component is an image-domain denoiser inside an iterative forward-backward solver, while every forward step still enforces consistency with measured visibilities through the wide-field Fourier operator, weighting, noise whitening, and w-effect correction. It therefore provides a clean comparison with uSARA: data handling and the outer solver remain aligned while the handcrafted multi-basis proximal operator is replaced by a pre-trained DNN.

The study also exposes evaluation requirements for an end-to-end or hybrid ML imager. A controlled comparison should retain identical visibilities, weighting, operator accuracy, pixel scale, field size, calibration treatment, flux apertures, stopping rules, and hardware, then report visibility-domain residuals, effective resolution, diffuse-source completeness, calibrated photometric error, spectral-index uncertainty, and truth-based image metrics. This paper's real-data evidence supports AIRI's sensitivity to diffuse emission and computational advantage, but the changing hand-drawn apertures, unequal compute platforms, unvalidated flux scale, internally inconsistent denoiser-variation range, and lack of ground truth prevent its visual or flux comparisons from establishing absolute reconstruction fidelity.
